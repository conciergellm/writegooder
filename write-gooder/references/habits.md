# The habits (the faux pas list)

Open this file at stage 4, repair, and not before. Reading these phrases just before you write makes you more likely to write them. When a sentence shows a habit, don't patch it: cut it, or go back to its notes and answer the listener's question again.

Each one has how to spot it, why a reader notices it, a before and after, and the neighbour it tends to come back as once you remove it.

The pairs marked (article) come from a real article that was rewritten and that its owner then said sounded better. The others were written for this file to cover emails and replies, and nobody has signed them off, so trust the marked ones more.

## Sentence habits

### 1. A quoted phrase used as an adjective

Spot it: a phrase in quotation marks sitting in front of a noun. Nobody can say this aloud without making quote marks in the air.

Before (article): Most "best local LLM for a 3090" posts tell you what fits in 24 GB.

After (article): We're posting the numbers because when we were picking models, most of what we found online stopped at "does it fit in 24 GB".

The after still has a quote, but now it is what those posts said, placed where a person would say it. It also has a "we" who went looking.

Neighbour: the hyphen pile ("the best-local-LLM-for-a-3090 kind of post"). Same problem.

### 2. A sentence with no subject you can hear

Spot it: the sentence starts with "Fewer", "Most", "Others", "Some", "The rest" or "The same goes for", and only makes sense if you carry the noun over from the sentence before.

Before (article): Fewer tell you what the model can finish once it's loaded.

After (article): Along the way we kept a record of how fast each model was and whether it could get through a job with more than one step.

The complaint about other posts is gone. What is left is what we did, with a subject you can hear.

Neighbour: "Fewer of them tell you". The subject is back but the sentence is still half of a setup and turn (habit 3).

### 3. Setup and turn

Spot it: two sentences of matching build where the first one exists so the second can turn against it. "Most do X. Fewer do Y." "X tells you A. It doesn't tell you B." "On paper it's simple. In practice it isn't."

Before (article): Tokens per second tells you how fast a model talks. It doesn't tell you whether it can do a job. So we ran two checks:

After (article): We ran two sets of tasks. The first is eight simple questions that each need one tool call, things like what's on the calendar today. The second is six longer jobs where the model has to make a plan, call several tools and check its own result before it answers.

The lesson was cut. Describing the two tests makes the same point without a speech.

Neighbour: one sentence with "but" or "while" that keeps the same balance ("While tokens per second tells you X, it doesn't tell you Y"). If the first half is only there to be knocked down, cut it.

### 4. Contrast lines

Spot it: "X, not Y". "It isn't A, it's B." "Not just A, but B." "Less about A, more about B." Often in titles and at the end of paragraphs.

Before (article, the title): What one RTX 3090 can really do with a local LLM: measured, not guessed

After (article): What one RTX 3090 can do with a local LLM (our numbers)

Before: This isn't a tool for replacing editors. It's a tool for giving them their evenings back.

After: We built it for editors who already know what they want and lose their evenings to doing it by hand.

Why readers notice: the "not" half argues with something nobody said. Say what the thing is.

Neighbour: "rather than", "instead of", "as opposed to", "more than just". A real contrast is fine when someone really did believe the other thing, as in "We expected the 27B to be the better agent, but it took twice as long."

### 5. The short fragment for effect

Spot it: a sentence of two to five words placed after longer ones, or two of them in a row, written to land. Also a fragment that answers a header.

Before (article): **3. Bigger wasn't faster.** The 27B across two cards passed the same jobs but took about twice as long.

After (article): We expected the 27B on two cards to be the better agent. It passed the same six jobs but took roughly twice as long, and on our day-to-day jobs the 35B came out about 40% faster.

Before (article): If you only chat, it's fine. If you want an agent, it isn't.

After (article): For chat that's fine. For anything agent-like we stopped using it.

Before (article, under the header "What a second 3090 is good for"): Not a bigger model, in our case.

After (article): So the second card now mostly sits free for Premiere and After Effects while the model runs on the first.

Before (article): All on the local model.

After (article): cut. The paragraph had already said so.

Short sentences are allowed ("We only have that figure for those two"). The test is whether it gives information or strikes a pose.

Neighbour: the one-line paragraph. The fragment gets a line to itself and looks even more like a slogan.

### 6. Triplets

Spot it: three adjectives, three nouns or three clauses in a row where the count is three because three sounds finished. "Fast, private and reliable." "No setup, no accounts, no surprises."

Before: It's fast, private and simple to set up.

After: It runs on your own PC, so nothing you give it leaves the room. Setting it up took our testers about ten minutes.

(Use the after only if those facts are true. If you have no fact behind an adjective, cut the adjective.)

A list of three real things is fine ("an i9-13900K with 128 GB of RAM and two 3090s"). Ask whether there were really three, and whether each one would survive on its own.

Neighbour: two matched pairs ("fast and private, simple and cheap").

### 7. Em dashes and the colon reveal

Spot it: any em dash. A spaced hyphen or en dash doing the same job. A colon used to pause before a payoff ("The result: nothing worked." "Here's the catch: it needs two cards.").

Before: We tried the smaller model first — and it was a mistake.

After: We tried the smaller model first, which was a mistake because it failed half the longer jobs.

Why: the dash is a held breath before a reveal. In speech a person would say "because" or "which" and keep going.

Neighbour: semicolons, colons, a parenthesis, three dots. If the sentence still pauses for effect, it hasn't been rebuilt. A colon before an actual list is ordinary.

### 8. A question asked so you can answer it

Spot it: a question followed at once by its answer from the same writer. "The result?" "So what does this mean for you?" "Why does this matter?"

Before: So is one card enough? In our experience, yes.

After: One card has been enough for us.

A real question to the reader, one you want answered, is fine ("If you've run these on other hardware, post your numbers").

Neighbour: "You might ask whether one card is enough." Same thing with more words.

### 9. A thing doing a person's job

Spot it: an abstract noun or an object as the subject of a verb only people do. Numbers that "tell", speed that "decides", a table that "lives" somewhere, a feature that "lets you" or "empowers".

Before (article): An agent reads long instructions at every step, so prompt speed decides how a job feels far more than writing speed does.

After (article): One thing we underrated at the start is prompt speed. An agent re-reads a long set of instructions on every step, so how fast the card reads matters at least as much as how fast it writes.

Before (article): The full table lives at conciergellm.com/local-llm-rtx-3090, and we update it when we measure something new.

After (article): The table is also on our site and we'll update it there as we test more.

Put a person in the subject where you can: we noticed, we stopped using it, I'd pick.

Neighbour: the passive ("it was found that"). The person is still missing.

### 10. Announcing instead of saying

Spot it: a sentence that says something is coming, or labels the next sentence. "It's worth noting that." "Here's what we found." "Let's break it down." "The short answer is." "These are our numbers, with the method, so you can compare them with your own."

Before: It's worth noting that we only tested on Windows.

After: We only tested on Windows.

Delete the announcement and see whether anything is missing. Usually nothing is.

Neighbour: "One thing to mention:" or "Quick note:".

### 11. Telling the reader what they think

Spot it: "more than you'd think", "you might be wondering", "if you're like most people", "we've all been there", "whether you're a beginner or a pro".

Before (article): **4. Reading speed matters more than you'd think.**

After (article): One thing we underrated at the start is prompt speed.

The writer doesn't know what the reader thinks. The writer does know what they themselves got wrong, and saying that is more believable.

Neighbour: "surprisingly", "it turns out". Say who was surprised and by what ("The 8B surprised us a little").

### 12. Stock words

Spot it: delve, dive in, leverage, unlock, elevate, streamline, seamless, robust, powerful, crucial, vital, landscape, journey, game-changer, testament, navigate, harness, tapestry, realm, "in today's world", "at the end of the day", "when it comes to", and the sincerity words (honestly, truly, simply, really, genuinely) used to push.

Before: Our app seamlessly integrates with your existing workflow.

After: It opens Premiere and After Effects on your PC and works in the project you already have.

A stock word almost always stands where a fact should be. Find the fact. If there isn't one, cut the sentence.

Neighbour: a fancier word for the same nothing ("effortlessly", "frictionless").

### 13. The "-ing" tail

Spot it: a comma and then a verb ending in "-ing" that comments on the sentence it hangs from. "..., highlighting the need for", "..., making it ideal for", "..., ensuring that", "..., reflecting a broader". One study found the tuned models use this build two to five times as often as people do.

Before: The 8B failed half the longer jobs, highlighting the limits of small models for agent work.

After: The 8B failed half the longer jobs, so we stopped using it for agent work.

End the sentence when the fact ends. If the tail holds a second fact, give it its own sentence with its own subject, or join it with "so" or "and".

Neighbour: "which highlights", "and this shows". If the tail was only a comment on the fact, cut it.

### 14. Nouns where a verb would do

Spot it: words ending in "-tion", "-ment", "-ness", "-ity" carrying the action, usually with "of" after them. The models write this noun-heavy way about twice as often as people.

Before: The implementation of local models enables the elimination of per-token costs.

After: We run the model on the PC, so nobody pays per token.

Ask who did what, and write that with the person as subject and the action as the verb.

Neighbour: "the fact that we run it locally means". Still a noun phrase as the subject.

### 15. Dressed-up "is" and "has"

Spot it: "serves as", "stands as", "acts as", "functions as", "represents", "boasts", "features", "offers" where a person would say "is" or "has". Also "notes", "explains", "highlights" where a person would write "says".

Before: The second card serves as a dedicated resource for creative apps.

After: The second card is free for Premiere and After Effects.

Neighbour: "works as", "doubles as". Use them only when the thing really has two jobs.

### 16. The verdict line

Spot it: a short sentence that tells the reader how important the last sentence was. "That's what actually matters." "And that matters." "That's the real story." "That's the part nobody talks about." "This is where it gets interesting." "Let that sink in." Also "Honestly?" and "Here's the kicker" before a point, and "quietly" attached to a verb ("quietly became", "quietly changed").

Before: It finished all six jobs on one card. That's what actually matters.

After: It finished all six jobs on one card, which is the result we cared about because our app runs jobs like those all day.

If the thing matters, say to whom and why in the same sentence. If you can't, the line was only there for weight, so cut it.

Neighbour: "That's the important bit." "Which is the whole point."

### 17. Two words for one

Spot it: adjectives or nouns that arrive in pairs joined by "and" where one would do. "Clear and simple", "fast and reliable", "tools and workflows", "time and effort". The tuned models pair words like this about twice as often as people.

Before: It gives editors a fast and reliable way to handle repetitive and time-consuming tasks.

After: It does the repetitive jobs for editors.

Keep a pair when the two words mean different things and both are needed.

Neighbour: three words. See triplets.

### 18. Favourite words of the moment

The lists change with each model, and when one word is trained out another takes its place, so check for any word you notice yourself using in every piece. Known ones as of late 2026: quietly, matters, steady, practical, dependable, prioritize, genuinely, honest (as in "the honest answer"), load-bearing, surface (as a verb), unpack, lean into, landscape, navigate, "a testament to", "plays a key role", "it's important to note". Claude's own are genuinely, honest, load-bearing and surface.

A word from this list is fine once where it is the plain word for the thing. It is a habit when it shows up because it sounds good.

### 19. The same skeleton again and again

Spot it: strip the words and look at the build of each sentence. Subject, verb, object, comma, "and" clause, five times in a row. Or every sentence between eight and twenty words. Studies of generated text find the same sentence skeletons repeating far more than in human text, and people who read a lot of it name "predictable sentence structure" as the second biggest giveaway after vocabulary.

Fix: this comes right when the listener pass is done properly, because speech has questions, asides, a very long sentence when the thought is long and a short one when it is short. Don't shuffle lengths on purpose. Retell the paragraph and let the content set the build.

Neighbour: a deliberate long, short, long, short pattern.

## Shape habits

Look at these with the page held away from you, as shapes.

### 20. Bold lead-ins and numbered takeaways

Spot it: every list item or paragraph starts with a bold phrase, often a little headline with a full stop, often numbered. "**1. One card is enough.** The 35B fits..."

Before (article):

**1. The small model is fast and wrong on anything with steps.** The 8B answered every quick question correctly and failed half the multi-step jobs. If you only chat, it's fine. If you want an agent, it isn't.

After (article):

The 8B surprised us a little. It got all eight simple questions right and was the quickest by a wide margin, but it only finished three of the six longer jobs. For chat that's fine. For anything agent-like we stopped using it.

The bold line was a conclusion handed over before the evidence. The after tells what happened and lets the reader conclude.

Neighbour: italic lead-ins, "First," "Second," "Third,", or a colon after the first few words of each paragraph.

### 21. A header over every paragraph

Spot it: count headers and count paragraphs. If the numbers are close, there are too many headers. Also headers that are sentences, questions or teasers.

Before (article): "Two kinds of test", "What we took from it", "What that feels like in real work", "What a second 3090 is good for", "What we haven't measured"

After (article): "Setup", "Results", "What we noticed", "In daily use", "Gaps"

Nine sections became five, and the headers became labels of a word or two. A reply or an email usually has none.

Neighbour: bold lines standing alone as headers.

### 22. Bullets where there is a story

Spot it: bullets whose items are sentences with reasons in them, or steps that happened in order. Bullets cut out the "because" and "so" between things.

Before (article):

- **Quick check:** eight one-step questions with one tool call each (what's on the calendar, what's the weather).
- **Multi-step check:** six jobs that need a plan, several tools and a checked result.

After (article): We ran two sets of tasks. The first is eight simple questions that each need one tool call, things like what's on the calendar today. The second is six longer jobs where the model has to make a plan, call several tools and check its own result before it answers.

Keep bullets and tables for things that are lists and tables: parts, prices, measurements. The article kept its results table.

Neighbour: a paragraph that is still a list ("First there's the quick check. Then there's the multi-step check.").

### 23. Paragraphs all the same size

Spot it: every paragraph is three or four sentences, and each ends on its strongest line. From a distance the page looks like bricks.

Fix: let content set the size. In the article after the rewrite, one paragraph is a single sentence listing the three models and the one after it is three sentences. If a paragraph's last sentence only restates the paragraph, cut it.

Neighbour: alternating long, short, long, short on purpose. That is a pattern too.

### 24. Openers

Spot it: a greeting to nobody, praise for the question, the question said back, a scene-setting line about the state of the world, "I hope this email finds you well", "I wanted to reach out".

Before (a forum reply): Great question! Whether you need a NAS for video editing really depends on your workflow. Let's break it down.

After: I'd get one. I edited off loose USB drives for two years and switched after I lost a project to a dead drive.

Before (an email): I hope this email finds you well. I wanted to reach out regarding the invoice for the March shoot.

After: The invoice for the March shoot is attached. It's the same amount as the quote.

Start with the answer or with who you are and why you're writing.

Neighbour: "Short answer:" followed by the answer. Just give the answer.

### 25. Closing lines

Spot it: a last line that sums up, sells, turns the piece into a lesson, or offers more. "In short", "The bottom line", "And that makes all the difference", "Hope this helps", "Let me know if you have any questions", "Happy to share more".

Before (article): The full table lives at our site, and we update it when we measure something new.

After (article): We haven't timed a 4090 or a 5090, and we haven't tested 16 GB cards at all, so we can't say anything useful about those yet. If you've run these models on other hardware, post your numbers. We'd like to compare.

Cover the last sentence with your hand. If the piece is complete without it, delete it. Ending on a practical detail or on what you don't know is fine.

Neighbour: a closing question to drive replies ("What's your experience been?") when you don't want the answer.

### 26. The flat report

Spot it: this one appears after the other habits are fixed. Every fact from the brief is there, one per sentence, and the sentences start the same way: "It did this. It also did that. We tried the other as well."

Before (a reply to someone asking whether a standing desk is worth the money): I bought a standing desk in March. It cost 400 dollars. It has two motors. It goes from 70 to 120 cm. I also bought a mat for it. I stand for about two hours a day.

After: I'd buy it again. I got mine in March and I stand for about two hours a day, mostly in calls. Get a mat with it, because without one my feet hurt inside twenty minutes.

The after drops true figures the asker didn't need and keeps the one comparison that answers them. Choosing is what makes it sound like someone who knows the subject.

Neighbour: variety for its own sake, where sentence openings are shuffled but the piece still recites everything.

### 27. Sure about everything

Spot it: no sentence in the piece admits a gap, or else every sentence is hedged with "may", "can", "often", "typically".

Before (article): 16 GB cards. We expect quick answers to be fine and long jobs to be less reliable, but that's an estimate.

After (article): We haven't timed a 4090 or a 5090, and we haven't tested 16 GB cards at all, so we can't say anything useful about those yet.

Also (article): We only have that figure for those two.

People who did the work know exactly where their knowledge stops and say it once, plainly, in the place it applies. Don't guess to fill the gap, and don't spread soft hedges over the things you do know.

Neighbour: a disclaimer paragraph at the end ("Of course, your results may vary").

## Humour habits

### 28. The joke shape

Spot it: a joke built from a template with the subject dropped in. "Plot twist:", "said no one ever", "X has entered the chat", "my wallet is crying", "tell me you're X without telling me", "we love to see it", "not me doing X", "the audacity", "this is fine", a pun on the subject's name, a list of three where the third is the joke, a fake headline, and slang the owner never uses ("no cap", "it's giving", "bestie", "slay"). Also a joke that explains itself, a "lol" or "😂" after its own punchline, and a run of jokes where every line is one.

Before (a friend posts a photo of the burnt lasagne they made for their in-laws): Plot twist: the lasagne was the real guest of honour 😂 Chef's kiss, bestie!

After: did they eat the corner piece or did you hide it

The before would work under any photo of any burnt dish. The after plays on the one thing in this photo, a black corner, and on the in-laws, and it's how a friend writes to a friend. A joke a person makes is about something only this post has.

Fix: cut the shape and look at the post again. Find the detail that is odd, sad or too much, and say the owner's reaction to that, made bigger or smaller than it was. If nothing in the post or the notes is funny, write the plain reply and say at hand-over that you had nothing to joke about.

Neighbour: the same shape with fresher words. A new catchphrase is still a catchphrase.
