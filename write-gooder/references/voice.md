# Voice

"Sound like this person" is not something a model can follow. A short list of things that can be counted is. There are three levels. Use the strongest one the material allows, and say at hand-over which one you used.

## Level 1: their own words

The person said it or wrote it roughly themselves: a voice note, a transcript, a rough paragraph, a string of messages. This is the best material there is, because nothing has to be rewritten and so nothing can turn into a model's voice.

Your job is to cut and to order. You write nothing new.

Allowed:

- Cut false starts, repeats, filler ("um", "you know", "like I said") and anything the listener doesn't need.
- Move whole sentences so the opening rule holds and each thing is introduced before it is used.
- Join two of their sentences with a short joining word.
- Fix what a speech-to-text tool misheard, and add punctuation.
- Fix a slip that would be misread: a wrong name, a wrong number, a missing word.

Not allowed:

- Swapping their word for a better one.
- Smoothing a sentence that is clear and sounds like talk.
- Adding a sentence, an opening or a closing of your own.
- Correcting grammar that is how they speak.

Still do stage 1's setup block, because you need the intention to know what to cut and the STARTS WITH line to order the opening. Skip stage 2's Talk step. Then go to stage 3 as usual. In stage 4, a marked sentence is cut or moved, or the person is asked. It is not redone by you.

If their rough version is missing something the listener needs, that is a question for them.

## Level 2: a voice card

Use this when you have a few things the person wrote or said (five to ten is plenty, and transcripts of them talking are the best kind) but not this piece in their words.

Read the samples and fill in the card. Every line is something you can count or point at. Quote an example for each from the samples.

```
VOICE CARD for: <name>          made from: <what samples>          date:
Sentence length:   usual range in words, and the longest they go
Openings:          how they start a message or a paragraph (straight in, a name, "so", a fact)
Shortened words:   do they write "it's", "don't", "we're"? always, sometimes, never
I or we:           which they use, and for what
Words they use:    five to ten words or phrases that turn up more than once
Words they don't:  words the samples never contain that you would reach for
How blunt:         do they say a thing is bad, or soften it? quote one
How they doubt:    how they say they aren't sure ("I think", "not sure but", "probably")
How they end:      where they stop (after the ask, mid-thought, with thanks)
Punctuation:       anything regular (no semicolons, lots of full stops, lower case)
Never:             anything they have said they don't want
```

Then write in those traits, and after stage 2 count the same things in your text. If the card says sentences run 8 to 18 words and yours average 26, you have left the voice.

The card describes how they build sentences. It is not a store of their phrases. Don't paste their sayings into a new piece to make it sound like them.

With the person's yes, save the card as `voice_card_<name>.md` so the next piece starts from it. Add to it when they correct you ("I'd never say that" goes under Never).

## Level 3: no samples

Write as a plain, careful person saying it across a table to the listener. Shortened words as in speech. No slang and no performance.

Say at hand-over that this is what you did, and that a voice note or a few samples would let the next one sound like them.

## At every level

- `Q` notes, the owner's exact words, go into the text as they said them.
- The reader in stage 3 is told that a spoken style is intended.
- Voice is how the sentences are built. It never licenses a fact, a feeling or a story the person didn't give you.
