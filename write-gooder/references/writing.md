# Stage 2 in full: write it as the owner

Have the setup block and the notes in front of you, and this file. Nothing else. The text is made here, once. Everything after this stage only marks and repairs.

Contents: 1 Choose and weigh. 2 When the takes are thin. 3 The opening. 4 Talk. 5 Rules for a sentence. 6 Tables and lists. 7 Join. 8 The last sentence. 9 The title. 10 By kind of piece.

## 1. Choose and weigh

The notes are marked A, B and C against the intention. Length follows weight:

- An **A** note gets a sentence of its own, and a second one if there is a take or a witness note to go with it.
- A **B** note hangs off an A as a clause, or shares a sentence with another B.
- A **C** note gets a few words in passing, a cell in a table, or nothing.

This is what makes a person's writing uneven. They spend words where it matters to them and hurry through the rest. A text where every fact gets one sentence of the same size is a report, and readers hear it as a machine with the obvious habits removed.

Write down which notes you leave out. A person with a point leaves most things out, so with raw material expect to drop a third or more. Two cases where you won't reach that, and shouldn't force it:

- **The material was already chosen for you.** A brief that lists only what the listener needs has no C notes in it. Test this, don't assume it. A fact sheet is chosen for the company, and the listener asked one thing. Ask of every note: does this person need it for the decision in INTENTION? In a reply to someone choosing a graphics card, how long the product takes to write social posts is a C, however true it is.
- **The listener needs the settings to check your result.** Put them in a table or one plain line. They are kept, and they take almost no words.

If the only way to shorten is to cut the one story in the material, keep the story and cut a setting.

## 2. When the takes are thin

Count the `T`, `W`, `Q` and `R` notes. A paragraph of two or more sentences needs at least one of them, or it is a paragraph of facts. So these notes set the length of the piece:

- With three of them, write three short paragraphs and put the remaining facts in a table or a single plain line. Don't stretch three takes over eight paragraphs.
- Never make up a take to fill a paragraph. A take is the owner's. What you can add is reasoning: an `R` note, said as reasoning.
- **With no takes at all and nobody to ask,** which is common for a company reply written from a fact sheet: the piece is carried by `R` notes. Work out what the facts mean for this listener's decision, say it plainly with how sure the facts make you, and put the facts behind it. Keep it short. Say at hand-over that nothing in it is the owner's own view, and which question would change that.
- At hand-over, say which one or two `T?` or `W?` questions would do the most for the piece.

Four kinds of paragraph are exempt, because they aren't there to carry a view: the ask, a line of settings, the line that says who is talking and what they sell, and a greeting or sign-off.

A short piece that is all the owner's is better than a long one that is half yours.

## 3. The opening

The first sentence has two halves, old then new.

- The **old half** is the subject, named the way the listener would name it themselves. Take it from STARTS WITH.
- The **new half** is the most important new thing about it. That is the ANGLE.

For a forum of people who keep large photo libraries: "We timed three ways of backing up a photo library" is the old half, and "the cheapest one was also the quickest to restore from" is the new half.

How this plays out:

- **A reply to a question:** the question is the old half. Don't repeat it. Start with the answer. If they asked yes or no, the first word they want is yes or no. That answer is an `R` note: it rests on your facts, so say what it rests on ("On our tests, yes"), and don't open on a fact that only implies it.
- **An email to someone who knows the owner:** the old half is the thing you both know about ("the Thursday shoot").
- **A cold post to strangers:** the old half is the subject in the reader's own terms.

There is no fixed opening. Who is talking goes first only when the point depends on it. If the owner sells something the piece mentions, that is said early, in a few words, and it need not be the first sentence.

Don't open with a hook, a scene, a question or a compliment. Don't open with the angle alone either: a point with no old half makes the listener ask "what are we talking about?" and spend the next lines catching up. And don't open with two paragraphs of background before anything new arrives.

This is the same thing a news lead, a topic sentence and "bottom line up front" all teach.

## 4. Talk

The listener asks and the owner answers out loud. Answering a question pulls toward spoken language. Composing an article pulls toward essay language.

**Order of questions.**

1. The question the opening answers ("what happened?" or "what is this about?").
2. The listener's strongest doubt, early. Look at what LISTENER says they suspect, and let them ask it within the first three questions. A person who knows their audience meets the objection before it hardens. This also stops the piece falling into the order a template would use. "Early" gives way to sense: don't let it split an answer from the thing it belongs with, and an apology or the thing the listener is most afraid of can come before it.
3. After that, whatever this listener would ask next, drawn from this set:
   - What did you expect?
   - Why did that happen?
   - How do you know?
   - What did you see?
   - What don't you know?
   - Who are you to say?
   - What do you want from me?

You may add a question that isn't in the set if this listener would plainly ask it. Stop asking when the A notes are used up or the length in WHERE is reached, whichever comes first.

**Answers.** Two sentences at most per answer. If there is more to say, the listener asks another question. After every sentence put the notes it came from in brackets: `[F3, T1]`. When a worked-out note stands for several given ones, tag those too.

**Don't tell the listener what they lived through.** If STARTS WITH says they were there, a sentence that describes it back to them is cut to the one new thing in it.

## 5. Rules for a sentence

**Build it on the take.** Where there is a take, the take is the main clause and the fact hangs off it. "We'd assumed the bigger model would be the better agent, because it needs both cards" weighs something. "The bigger model needs both cards. It was slower." lists. One sentence that weighs two things is better than two that list them.

**No note, no sentence.** A sentence with no note behind it is filler: a summing up, a verdict, a bridge. Delete it.

**The swap test.** Could this sentence sit unchanged in a piece about a different subject? Then it is generic. Put a detail from the notes in it or delete it.

**Somebody does something.** Put a person in the subject where there is one. "We ran the tests" and not "The tests were conducted".

**Known before new.** Each sentence starts from something already said and ends on what is new. A name, a term or a thing can't be used before it has been introduced. Before keeping a sentence, list the things it mentions and check each has come up already or is explained right there. This covers the owner's jargon too: a word the listener doesn't use ("the profile", to someone who only knows "my projects") is said in their word for it. If no note says what it means in their terms, keep the owner's word and put the question in the hand-over.

**Cause words need a cause.** "So", "because", "since" and "which is why" claim that one thing led to another. Use one only when a note says so: a take, a reasoning note, or a given fact that states the cause. If nothing says why, use "and" or "then".

**Side by side means connected.** Two sentences next to each other are read as belonging together. If the notes don't connect them (a timing that may or may not be from the same setup), separate them or say what isn't known.

**Days and dates.** Check every "today", "tomorrow" and "by Wednesday" against the SENT line. If it could be read as two different days, write the date.

**One voice for I and we.** OWNER says which the owner uses. A company's work is "we". What one person thought, expected or forgot is "I". Don't drift between them, and don't turn an "I" into a "we" to sound bigger: "I kept no notes" is a person, "we kept no notes" is a press release.

**Their words stay theirs.** A `Q` note goes in as they said it.

**Short is allowed.** A short sentence that carries a fact or a take is how people talk. The one to avoid is the short sentence that carries nothing and is there to land.

**Don't say more than the notes.** If a note says "about 2 minutes" and doesn't say per job or in total, the sentence doesn't say either. Numbers in the text match the notes exactly.

**Joke when the piece is meant to be funny.** That is when the owner asks for funny, or when the place and the people call for it (a group chat, a reply to a friend's post, a toast) and the owner talks that way. Don't add a joke to a forum answer or a work email to seem warm.

A joke needs a note like any other sentence. A person's joke plays on something specific: a detail in the post they are answering, something this group knows, or the owner's real reaction made bigger or smaller than it was. A model's joke is a familiar joke shape with the subject dropped in, and readers spot that faster than any phrase. So the swap test is strictest here. If the joke would still work under a different post, it's a stock joke. Cut it, and find the detail it should have been about. If the joke needs shared history you don't have, ask for it. Say the joke once, plainly, and don't explain it or point at it.

**Slang only if it's theirs.** Slang, swearing, abbreviations and lower case come in when the owner's samples, their `Q` notes or the thread they're answering use them. Never add slang to sound young or relaxed. Borrowed slang is the costume readers notice first, and it fools only the readers who weren't paying attention.

**Don't perform.** No "honestly", no "look,", no typos, no made-up aside. A sentence put there to show a personality reads as a costume, in a serious piece and a funny one alike.

## 6. Tables and lists

Three or more rows of numbers that the listener will compare go in a table, in a forum reply too where the forum shows tables. Fewer than that reads better as a sentence. A real list of parts stays a list. A table is one answer, to "show me the numbers", and its note tags go on one line under it.

A cell with no note behind it says "not measured". It is never filled in.

Prose doesn't read the table back. A sentence about a row in the table has to add what the owner makes of that row, or the reasoning from it, or it is cut. The opening may state the one number the answer rests on, even though the table has it too.

Bullets are for lists. A story told in bullets loses its "because" and "so", which is most of the story.

## 7. Join

- Remove the questions.
- Add only short joining words between answers: and, but, so, because, although, since, when, then, which is why. The cause words follow the rule above.
- Add no new sentences.
- You may move a whole answer, or a sentence inside one, so that a reference sits next to what it points at. A "that" or "this" must have its thing in the sentence before.
- Break the paragraph where the listener changed the subject. Paragraphs come out different sizes because the notes had different weights. Leave them that way.
- **Look at how the paragraphs are built.** If every one is a short claim followed by a sentence of figures, the piece has one mould and the reader will see it by the third paragraph. Let one paragraph be a single sentence, put the figures in the table, and give the paragraph that matters most the extra sentence.
- **Skim check.** Read only the first sentence of every paragraph. Together they should give the piece in small. If one of them doesn't say what its paragraph is about, move a sentence up. Use this as a check and never as a mould: a paragraph built as one claim and three supports is the school essay.
- Keep the note tags until hand-over.

## 8. The last sentence

It is the last thing the listener needs. Often that is the ask.

If it is an ask, the listener must be able to do it with what the text gave them. "Send me your numbers" fails when the piece never said which numbers. Name what you want back. Before the ask, the listener's fear about doing it has been answered if a note answers it ("installing over the old one keeps your projects"); if no note does, that is the first open question at hand-over.

When there is no ask, as in most replies, stop on the last thing the listener needs for their decision. Don't make up an ask to have an ending.

If the piece is complete without its last sentence, delete that sentence. Don't sum up, don't draw a lesson, and don't offer more help.

## 9. The title

Written last, from the notes, and only if the piece has one (a reply has none). A title or subject line is a plain label of what the piece is about, in the listener's words for it. It isn't a sentence, it has no colon with a payoff after it, and it doesn't try to carry the angle and its caveats. "Backup speeds for three photo library tools" is a title. It carries no note tags. In the tagged file write an email's subject as `Subject: ...` on the first line.

## 10. By kind of piece

**Article or post.** Headers only if it is long, and then a few labels of a word or two ("Setup", "Results"). Say early if the owner sells something. Tell it in the order it happened once the opening is done, including what didn't go to plan and the figure you don't have.

**Reply on a forum or thread.** The first sentence is the answer. No greeting, no headers, no bold, no bullets unless you are listing items. Say how the owner knows. A few short paragraphs of different sizes. Stop without offering more.

**Email.** The first line says what the email is for. One ask, as a plain request, with a date if there is one. When there is news and an ask, the news goes first and the ask gets its own short paragraph at the end. A subject line that is a label. A greeting and sign-off as plain as the owner would use, and say at hand-over that those two are yours.

**Product page.** The listener is deciding whether this is for them. Old half: the problem in their words. New half: what this does about it. Then what it costs them in money and effort, what it can't do, and how to get it. Features go in a list, and every claim has a fact note behind it.

**Social post.** One point. The opening rule matters most here because there is only an opening. No hashtags or emoji unless the owner uses them.

**Group chat, or a reply to friends.** One to three lines. The post they're answering is the old half, so don't restate it. React to it. No greeting, no sign-off, no paragraph. Write the way the group writes: their punctuation, their capitals, their emoji or none. The setup and notes can be a few lines: LISTENER is the group, STARTS WITH is the post, and the notes are the details in it and anything the owner told you about these people. When asked for options, give three to five that each play on a different detail or take a different line on it, such as agreeing far too hard, a flat understatement, or a callback the group would get. One joke reworded five times is one option. In the hand-over, say in a few words what each option plays on, so the owner can pick the one that fits the group.

**White paper or other long document.** Run the Talk step once per section, each with its own question from the listener. Section headers are labels. The summary at the top follows the opening rule for the whole document: what the reader already knows about the problem, then the one new thing this paper shows. Say plainly what is built and what is only planned, and keep them in different sections. Evidence is given with how it was measured and how small the sample was.
